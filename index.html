01 / FOUNDATIONS

Learn the language of charts

Understand the building blocks before studying setups.

Candlesticks (OHLC)

Each candle shows open, high, low, and close for one time interval. The body shows the open-to-close range; the wicks show the highest and lowest traded prices.

Timeframes

One candle can represent 1 minute, 5 minutes, 1 hour, 4 hours, 1 day, or 1 week. Higher timeframes show broader structure; lower timeframes contain more noise.

ScalpingDay tradingSwing tradingPosition trading

Trend structure

Uptrend: higher highs (HH) and higher lows (HL). Downtrend: lower highs (LH) and lower lows (LL). A range moves between boundaries.

Support & resistance

Support is an area where buyers previously became active. Resistance is an area where sellers previously became active. Think zones, not exact magic prices.

Volume

Volume measures how much traded during a period. Rising volume during a breakout may show stronger participation, but it cannot guarantee direction.

Liquidity & volatility

Liquidity is how easily an asset can trade without moving price much. Volatility is the size and speed of price changes. Both affect execution risk.

02 / MARKET TYPES

Different markets, different risks

Know what the instrument represents and how it trades.

🏢 Stocks

Shares represent ownership in a company. Prices respond to earnings, expectations, interest rates, news, and overall market conditions.

EquitiesCompany risk

💱 Forex

Currency pairs compare one currency with another, such as EUR/USD. Forex is decentralized and commonly offered with leverage, which magnifies losses too.

Currency pairsMacro data

₿ Crypto

Digital assets trade around the clock on many venues. Volatility, custody, exchange risk, liquidity, and regulation all matter.

24/7High volatility

🛢 Commodities

Oil, gold, and agricultural products can react to supply, demand, inventories, weather, geopolitics, and currency changes.

Physical marketsSupply / demand

📈 Indices

An index tracks a group of securities, such as the S&P 500 or Nasdaq-100. Exposure is usually through funds or derivatives, not by buying the index itself.

Market basketBroad exposure

📜 Futures & options

Derivatives get value from an underlying asset. They have contract rules, expiration, margin, and can produce rapid losses. Learn mechanics before considering them.

AdvancedComplex risk

05 / ADVANCED CONCEPTS

How experienced chart readers think

Use several pieces of evidence instead of relying on one indicator.

Break of structure (BOS)

Price breaks a prior swing point in the direction of the current move. A candle close beyond the swing can be more meaningful than a brief wick through it.

Change of character (CHOCH)

A possible early warning that the prior swing sequence is weakening—for example, an uptrend breaking a meaningful higher low. Not proof of reversal.

Moving averages

Moving averages smooth price over selected periods. Traders study slope, price location, and crossovers. They lag because they use past prices.

RSI

The Relative Strength Index is a momentum oscillator from 0 to 100. Above 70 and below 30 are often called overbought and oversold, but can persist in strong trends.

MACD

MACD compares moving averages to visualize momentum. Crossovers and histogram changes are context clues, not standalone buy or sell commands.

Fibonacci retracement

Common levels include 38.2%, 50%, and 61.8%. They are possible areas of interest after a clear swing, not guaranteed reversal prices.

Liquidity sweep

Price moves beyond a visible swing high or low where stop orders may cluster, then returns. It can signal rejection, but needs confirmation and context.

False breakout

Price crosses a range boundary and then returns inside. Watch whether price can hold beyond the level and whether a retest succeeds.

Multi-timeframe analysis

Use a higher timeframe for context, a middle timeframe for the setup, and a lower timeframe for detailed observation. Avoid letting tiny moves override the bigger picture.

08 / PROFESSIONAL PROCESS

Build a complete trading process

Strong analysis is a repeatable process. It is not a prediction contest.

LESSON 01 · MARKET STRUCTURE

Read the auction, not just the candles

Price moves because buyers and sellers continuously negotiate. A chart is a simplified record of that auction. Start by identifying whether the market is trending, balancing in a range, or transitioning between the two.

  • Mark obvious swing highs and lows; do not label every tiny wiggle.
  • In an uptrend, watch whether pullbacks hold above prior meaningful lows.
  • In a downtrend, watch whether rallies fail below prior meaningful highs.
  • A structure break is more meaningful when price closes beyond a swing and follow-through appears.

Practice question: If price makes a higher high but then breaks below the last important higher low, what changed? The prior bullish structure may be weakening; wait for more evidence before calling a reversal.

LESSON 02 · MULTI-TIMEFRAME ANALYSIS

Top-down chart reading

Use a higher timeframe to understand context, a middle timeframe to study the setup, and a lower timeframe only to inspect detail. For example, a learner might compare daily, 1-hour, and 5-minute charts. These are examples, not a prescribed trading system.

  • Higher timeframe: trend, major range, important levels.
  • Middle timeframe: swing structure, consolidation, breakout attempt.
  • Lower timeframe: candle behavior around the level.
  • If timeframes conflict, record the conflict instead of forcing a trade idea.

Lower timeframes contain more noise and transaction costs can matter more. More screen time does not automatically create an edge.

LESSON 03 · VOLUME & PARTICIPATION

Understand volume in context

Volume is the amount traded during a selected interval. Compare it with recent volume rather than treating a single large bar as a signal.

  • Breakout plus expanding volume can indicate broader participation.
  • A move on unusually light volume may be easier to reverse, but not always.
  • High volume at a turning area can represent aggressive buying or aggressive selling; price response helps interpret it.
  • Volume differs across markets and venues. Spot forex volume is not a complete centralized market total.

Ask: did price travel far for the amount of volume, or did heavy activity produce little movement? That relationship can be useful context.

LESSON 04 · INDICATORS

Indicators are transformations of price

Most common indicators are calculated from historical price and/or volume. They summarize information; they do not know the future.

  • Moving average: smooths price to make direction easier to inspect, but lags.
  • RSI: summarizes recent momentum. Overbought does not automatically mean sell; oversold does not automatically mean buy.
  • MACD: compares moving averages to show momentum shifts; it can whipsaw in ranges.
  • ATR: estimates recent range/volatility and can help compare movement across instruments.

Avoid stacking many indicators that all measure similar things. A clean chart and a clear question are often more useful.

LESSON 05 · BREAKOUTS & RETESTS

Separate a real break from a trap

A breakout is a move beyond a defined support, resistance, or range boundary. A retest is a return toward that boundary after the break.

  • Define the level before price reaches it.
  • Observe whether the candle closes outside the level, not only whether a wick crosses it.
  • Look for follow-through or a retest that holds.
  • Decide what would invalidate the breakout idea.

False breakouts happen. A setup is incomplete if the trader cannot explain where the idea is wrong.

LESSON 06 · MARKET REGIMES

Strategies behave differently in different conditions

A trend-following idea may struggle in a sideways range. A mean-reversion idea may struggle when a strong trend keeps extending. This is called regime dependence.

  • Trending: directional swing structure and sustained movement.
  • Ranging: repeated movement between boundaries.
  • High volatility: larger and faster price changes.
  • Low volatility: compressed movement; sudden expansion is possible.

Before evaluating a strategy, define the market conditions it is intended for—and the conditions where it should stand aside.

Study method: Pick one concept, find 20 historical examples, label them before looking at what happened next, then review the misses as carefully as the successes. This reduces hindsight bias.

10 / REFERENCE DESK

Trading glossary

Keep these terms close while studying charts and the simulator.

BidHighest displayed price a buyer is offering.
AskLowest displayed price a seller is offering.
SpreadDifference between bid and ask.
LiquidityHow readily an asset can trade with limited price impact.
VolatilityDegree of price variation over time.
SlippageDifference between expected and actual fill price.
Market orderOrder intended to execute promptly at available prices.
Limit orderOrder with a specified maximum buy or minimum sell price.
Stop orderOrder activated when a trigger price is reached; execution price can differ.
DrawdownDecline from an account's prior peak.
ExpectancyAverage expected result per trade over a sufficiently large sample.
Win rateShare of trades that close with a positive result.
Profit factorGross profits divided by gross losses over a sample.
R-multipleTrade result expressed as a multiple of initial planned risk.
BacktestApplying defined rules to historical data.
OverfittingRules tuned too closely to past data and unlikely to generalize.
FOMOFear of missing out; can lead to impulsive entries.
Hindsight biasBelieving an outcome was more predictable after it occurred.
CorrelationHow two variables move in relation; not proof of causation.
Market regimeCurrent broad condition, such as trend, range, or high volatility.
ConfluenceSeveral independent clues supporting one analysis.
